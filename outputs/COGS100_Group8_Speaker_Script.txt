Hi everyone, I’m Javen Cao. We’re Group 8, and today we’re talking about Bruner’s paper on Piaget and Vygotsky. We’ll use one simple question: how does a mind grow? Piaget and Vygotsky both care about that question, but they answer it in very different ways.

The quiz link is on this slide. Every answer shows up in our presentation.

Before we compare the two theories, let’s start with Bruner’s question: what is special about human thinking?

SLIDE 2
JAVEN — about 1 minute 30 seconds

Bruner starts with a simple point. Lots of living things adapt to the world around them. Humans do too. But humans also do something extra: we make theories and stories about the world. We make stories about other people. We even make stories about ourselves.

That matters because stories and theories help us explain things, argue with each other, and work together. Sometimes they even give people things to fight about.

So the big development question is this: how does a child grow into someone who can build and share these kinds of accounts? Piaget gives one answer. Vygotsky gives another.

SLIDE 3
JAVEN — about 1 minute 20 seconds

Both thinkers agree on one starting point. We do not just take in the world like a camera. The mind sits between what happens out there and what we come to know.

Piaget’s big insight is logic-like operations. He thinks the child slowly builds better ways to organize experience. Vygotsky’s big insight is different. He thinks human culture and history give us tools for thinking, especially language.

So this is not only a disagreement about teaching. It is a disagreement about what should be at the center of a theory of mind. Let’s start with Piaget.

SLIDE 4
JAVEN — about 2 minutes

Piaget says the mind uses organized operations to make sense of experience. He does not mean that the mind is literally a calculator. He means that thinking has a structure.

For Piaget, knowledge is made, not simply found. A child acts in the world, organizes what happened, and then tests that understanding through more action. If the understanding works, it becomes part of a stronger way of thinking.

That is why the word “mediate” matters here. The world does not turn straight into knowledge. The child’s way of thinking helps shape what the child can know.

Those operations begin in action. Over time, they become more internal, less stuck to one immediate point of view, and more open to reflection. That leads to Piaget’s story about growth.

SLIDE 5
JAVEN — about 2 minutes

Here is Piaget’s basic sequence. First, a child does things: touching, moving, sorting, and trying things out. Later, the child can carry some of those actions in thought. Then thought becomes less tied to what is right in front of the child. Eventually, the person can think about thought itself.

Piaget calls the pressure behind this growth disequilibration. Imagine you meet something that does not fit what you already think. You can try to fit it into your old idea. That is assimilation. Or you can change the old idea. That is accommodation.

Piaget says the tension between those two moves pushes thinking forward. Bruner’s criticism is that the basic idea is clear, but the exact mechanism is still not fully explained.

SLIDE 6
JAVEN — about 2 minutes 20 seconds, then personal takeaway

Bruner gives Piaget a lot of credit. Piaget showed that development is not just a random pile of habits. It has a direction and a pattern. Bruner says one thing is very clear in Piaget’s theory: development follows an invariant course.

But another part is less clear. What actually moves a child from one stage to the next? Bruner thinks Piaget tells us more about where growth goes than about what causes it.

Bruner also thinks Piaget leaves out too much about other people. How do people understand each other well enough to teach, negotiate, and build a shared world? In Piaget’s account, culture often looks more like a challenge to the individual than a resource the individual can use.

One thing I learned from this section is that a theory can be great at showing the direction of change and still leave the engine of change unclear.

That gap brings us to Vygotsky.

SLIDE 7
JUQY — about 1 minute 40 seconds

Hi, I’m Juqy Chen. Vygotsky also thinks the mind stands between the world and our experience. But he does not start with logic. He starts with meaning.

For Vygotsky, meaning depends on language and on the culture where that language is used. So mental growth means learning the symbolic tools that a culture already has. Think about how algebra can reorganize arithmetic. It gives you a new way to handle earlier ideas.

This growth does not come from inside one child alone. It depends on cultural tools and on ongoing contact with other people. That leads to Vygotsky’s key question: how do tools that start outside us become tools inside our own thinking?

SLIDE 8
JUQY — about 2 minutes 10 seconds

For Vygotsky, mental life starts out social. People act together. They talk. They solve problems together. And speech carries shared meanings from the culture around them.

Internalization is the name for what happens next. Meanings that first show up between people become resources inside one person’s thinking.

This is not just copying someone else. Once a person has a new tool for thinking, that tool can change how older ideas fit together. It can also help the person go beyond the immediate situation.

Bruner points out that Vygotsky does not fully explain the mechanism of internalization either. But the main idea is clear: social life and cultural tools are not just outside influences. They can become part of the way a person thinks.

SLIDE 9
JUQY — about 2 minutes 10 seconds

The zone of proximal development, or ZPD, makes Vygotsky’s social view very concrete. It is the gap between what a learner can do alone and what that learner can do with useful help from someone else.

The point is not that any help works. Good help has to match the learner’s next possible step. A tutor might point out the key part of a problem, block distractions, break the task into steps, or help the learner talk through the problem.

The learner is still doing the learning. The tutor is not simply giving away the answer. The tutor notices what the learner can almost do and gives just enough support to make that next step possible.

For Bruner, this also raises a bigger question: humans teach each other in a systematic way. What makes teaching and being taught possible in the first place?

SLIDE 10
JUQY — about 2 minutes

Our learning outcome asks us to connect these ideas to cognitive science. This is a useful rough comparison, not a claim that either thinker fits perfectly into one modern label.

Piaget sounds closer to traditional cognitive science. He looks for general, logic-like structures inside an individual mind. He wants explanations that can be checked and tested.

Vygotsky sounds closer to situated cognitive science. He puts language, culture, history, and a particular interaction right in the middle of the story. He is more interested in how people make meaning in context.

But we should not turn this into a cartoon. Piaget did not say culture never matters. And Vygotsky did not say development has no movement from concrete ideas to more abstract ones. The real difference is where each theory puts the center of explanation.

SLIDE 11
JUQY — about 1 minute 45 seconds

Bruner does not think the answer is to blend Piaget and Vygotsky into one easy theory. He thinks a good theory of development has to explain two very different things.

First, why is development often stable? Why can good teaching fail to make an immediate difference? Why is it hard to transfer learning from one setting to another? Piaget helps us look for those regularities and limits.

Second, why can development sometimes move quickly? Why can the right kind of collaboration open up a new possibility? Vygotsky helps us look for those levers of change: scaffolding, joint activity, and cultural opportunity.

That is why Bruner thinks their disagreement is useful. Each theory pushes the other to answer questions it might otherwise ignore.

SLIDE 12
JUQY — about 1 minute, then personal takeaway

To close, Piaget helps us see how thinking can become more organized and systematic. Vygotsky helps us see how language, culture, and other people become part of thinking.

Bruner’s main point is not that one thinker wins. Their difference is valuable because it stops us from explaining development with only logic or only culture.

One thing I learned is that even private thought can have a social history. Tools like language first live between people, and then they can become tools inside one person’s mind.
