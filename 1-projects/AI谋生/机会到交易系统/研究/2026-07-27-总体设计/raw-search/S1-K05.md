## Introduction

E-commerce retail is a priority for companies and is growing at a tremendous rate (Vazquez, 2020) especially in the current COVID-19 situation where traditional face-to-face retail is a challenge. COVID-19 has nearly doubled e-commerce sales (Blake, 2020) which, in essence, is good news for the e-commerce sector. However, at the same time, the explosion of demand for online purchasing and delivery has put overwhelming pressure on e-retailers which has resulted in failures and mistakes in the delivery of online purchases, ranging from cancelling the orders (Stuff, 2020) to delivering the wrong products (Niall, 2020). Overall, failures in the electronic commerce process are frequent and common (Tan et al., 2016; Goode et al., 2017). In the UK alone in 2017, customers registered nearly 12 million formal complaints about retailers, giving the retail sector the title of “worst performing sector” (25% of total complaints) (Uk-Ombudsman, 2018).

In practice, service failure is largely unavoidable, especially when the pressure on the business is high, but the art of satisfactory recovery plays a substantial role in rectifying customers’ dissatisfaction and negative attitudes which in turn leads to higher customer retention (Tan et al., 2016; Babin et al., 2020; Crisafulli and Singh, 2017). Market studies have noted a significant improvement (from 67% to 40%) in customer drop rate in situations where a service failure is adequately rectified by the provider (as perceived by the affected customer) (Knox and Van Oest, 2014). Therefore, it is crucial to explore the best mechanism through which retailers could most effectively redress a service failure to reduce negative consequences as much as possible. This forms the focus of the current paper.

More precisely, the current study is inspired by two gaps in the existing knowledge. The first gap is rooted in the type, or phase, of e-retailing failures. The failures in e-retailing occur in a variety of forms ranging from infrastructure outage and website navigation errors to privacy breach, security issues, faulty products, and poor delivery (Tan et al., 2016). --------------------------------------------------------------------------------

# The perils of proactive churn prevention using plan recommendations: Evidence from a field experiment

## Abstract

Facing the issue of increasing customer churn, many services have begun recommending pricing plans to their customers. One reason behind this type of retention campaign is that customers who are on a tariff suitable for them should be less likely to churn as they derive greater benefits from the service. In this paper, the authors examine the effectiveness of such retention campaigns using a large-scale field experiment in which some customers were offered plan recommendations and some were not. They find that being proactive and encouraging customers to switch to cost-minimizing plans can, surprisingly, increase rather than decrease customer churn: Whereas only 6% of customers in the control condition churned during the three months following the intervention, 10% did so in the treatment group. The authors propose two explanations for how the campaign increased churn, namely, by lowering customers' inertia to switch plans and by increasing the salience of past usage patterns among potential churners. The data provide support for both explanations. By leveraging the richness of our field experiment, the authors assess the impact of targeted encouragement campaigns on customer behavior and firm revenues and derive recommendations for service firms.
